Peak Season Fulfillment Planning: A Q4 Playbook

Peak is won or lost weeks before Black Friday — in the forecast, the inbound schedule and the shipping deadlines you publish.

Published 2026-11-02 · 7 min read

Peak season fulfillment planning comes down to three things decided weeks before the rush: enough stock on the shelf, a forecast your warehouse has actually seen, and shipping deadlines your customers know about. Brands that get those right have a busy December. Brands that don't spend it apologising for late orders.

This is the plan we would want every brand to have in hand before Black Friday, laid out in the order the decisions need to be made. If you are reading it in November, skip to the section that matches how many weeks you have left — most of it is still recoverable.

Start from the dates that cannot move

Work backwards from the last day a customer can order and still receive a gift on time. Everything else in your plan — reorder dates, inbound shipments, promotion timing — hangs off that date and the shipping deadlines that come before it.

Three dates matter most:

  • Your last inbound date. The last day stock can reach the warehouse and still be checked in, counted and sellable before your biggest week. Leave a buffer: a truck that arrives on the Monday of Cyber Week competes with everybody else's.
  • Your last guaranteed ship date by service level. Ground, two-day and overnight each have a different cut-off for Christmas delivery. Carriers publish their holiday deadlines every autumn; take them from the carrier, not from last year's email.
  • The days your 3PL is not shipping. Ask directly which days around Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year the warehouse operates. A shipping calendar that assumes every weekday is a working day will promise customers something you cannot deliver.

From our Houston warehouse, orders that reach us by 2 PM Central on a business day ship the same day. That is the number to build your storefront messaging around — an order placed at 4 PM on a Friday goes out on Monday, and your "order by" banner should say so.

Forecast in units, by SKU, by week

A revenue target is not a fulfillment forecast. Your warehouse plans labor and space around units and orders, so give them units and orders.

The simplest useful forecast takes last year's peak weeks, SKU by SKU, and adjusts each one for what has changed: growth since then, a bigger email list, a new channel, a promotion you are running that you did not run before. If you have no prior peak to work from, use your best recent month and scale it by the lift you expect from your promotions — and be honest about the uncertainty rather than hiding it in one confident number.

Two details make a forecast far more useful to the people packing the boxes:

  • Orders, not just units. A thousand units shipped as a thousand single-item orders is a very different day from a thousand units shipped as 250 bundles.
  • The spike, not just the total. If half your November volume will land in the 48 hours after a Black Friday email, say so. A warehouse can staff for a spike it knows about.

Share the forecast with your 3PL early, then update it when your plans change. A forecast nobody at the warehouse has seen is a wish list.

Get the stock in before everyone else does

Stockouts during peak are rarely a sales problem. They are usually a receiving problem: the inventory exists, but it is sitting on a dock, in a container yard or on a truck while the orders come in.

Plan inbound in waves rather than one big delivery. Bring your fast movers in first, with enough cover to get through your heaviest weeks, and follow with the rest. Ask your 3PL how long check-in takes once a delivery is at the dock during their busiest weeks — that is a different number from the one they quote in March.

Space is the other half of this. Peak inventory is temporary, and paying for it all year makes no sense. Our Houston warehousing is billed for the space you actually occupy, so you can scale up for a Q4 push and back down in the quiet months. At roughly $18 per pallet per month for standard storage, a few extra pallets for eight weeks is a small line next to the cost of selling out of your best SKU in early December.

If your peak stock is on a ship

Importers carry the biggest timing risk, because ocean freight leaves the least room to recover from a delay. A container that clears customs and then waits for a trucker and a dock slot during the busiest weeks of the year can lose days you do not have — and accrue per-diem and detention while it waits.

This is where a port-direct setup earns its keep. We dray containers from the Port of Houston and devan them at our own dock, aiming to count and shelve the stock the same day it arrives, so DTC orders can ship within about 48 hours of the vessel clearing. Send vessel details as soon as you have them so the dray is booked against the free-time window, not after it.

Build bundles and gift sets before the rush

Holiday gift sets, bundles and limited-edition boxes are great for average order value and hard on a warehouse floor, because every one of them is several picks and an assembly step. Doing that assembly order by order in the busiest week of the year is how mistakes and delays happen.

Build them in advance instead. A kitting run turns components into finished, barcoded units that sit on the shelf as their own SKU, so a gift-set order ships as a single pick. We scale labor to the build, which is what makes a large seasonal run on a deadline realistic. The constraint is on your side: components need to arrive with enough time to assemble them before the orders do.

Set customer expectations you can keep

Most peak-season complaints are not about slow shipping. They are about shipping that was slower than the customer was told.

  • Publish your holiday ordering deadlines on the site, in the cart and in your promotional emails, by shipping method.
  • Turn off ground shipping as a Christmas option once its deadline has passed, rather than letting customers choose a service that cannot get there in time.
  • Check your carrier settings. Carriers commonly add peak-season surcharges, so make sure the rates your checkout shows reflect what you will actually pay.

Location helps here more than people expect. Ground service from Houston reaches roughly 60% of the US population in one to two days, so for a large share of your customers the ground deadline sits only a day or two before the faster, more expensive services — fewer orders need upgrading to arrive on time. Our carriers are selected across USPS, UPS and FedEx by cost and speed for each order.

Plan for the exceptions, because there will be more of them

Volume multiplies everything, including the things that go wrong. At 99.8% pick accuracy, two orders in every thousand still need putting right — and in a peak week there are a lot more thousands. Agree before November how a mis-pick is reshipped and who pays, how a lost parcel claim is filed, and who you contact when an order needs to go out after the cut-off.

Set low-stock alerts on your top sellers in the inventory portal, and decide in advance what you will do when one fires: pause the ad, switch the product to pre-order, or point shoppers to an alternative. Making that call at 9 PM on Cyber Monday is harder than making it now.

If you are short on time

With four weeks or less until your peak, do not try to change everything. Focus on the three moves that protect the most revenue: get your top 10 to 20 SKUs into the warehouse and checked in, publish your holiday shipping deadlines, and send your 3PL a forecast — even a rough one — for the peak week.
