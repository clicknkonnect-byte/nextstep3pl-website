DIM Weight and Shipping Zones Explained

Your postage is billable weight looked up against a zone. Here is how each is set and what you can change.

Published 2026-11-23 · 6 min read

Dimensional (DIM) weight is a carrier's way of billing a parcel by the space it takes up instead of what it weighs, and a shipping zone is a distance band that sets the price per pound. Your postage is the billable weight looked up against the zone, so you can lower it by shrinking the box and by shipping from a location that puts your customers closer.

Both rules are public, and both are easy to ignore until a carrier invoice arrives. Here is how each one works, with the arithmetic shown, and what you can change about them.

What DIM weight is

A truck, a plane and a sorting hub all have a limited amount of room. A light but bulky box uses as much of that room as a dense heavy one, so carriers do not bill on scale weight alone. They calculate two numbers and charge you for the larger:

  • Actual weight: what the packed parcel weighs on a scale.
  • Dimensional weight: length × width × height in inches, divided by a number called the DIM divisor.

The larger of the two is the billable weight, and it is rounded up to the next whole pound. The divisor is set by each carrier and has changed over the years. For commercial domestic shipping, a divisor of 139 is common for UPS and FedEx, while USPS applies DIM weight to larger parcels using 166. Treat those numbers as a starting point and read the terms in your own carrier agreement, because they are exactly the kind of detail that gets revised.

A worked example

Say you ship a lightweight product, such as a knit throw, that weighs 2 lb once packed. Compare two boxes, using a divisor of 139:

  • 16 × 12 × 10 in box: 1,920 cubic inches ÷ 139 = 13.8, rounded up to 14 lb billable. You pay for 14 lb to ship a 2 lb parcel.
  • 12 × 10 × 8 in box: 960 cubic inches ÷ 139 = 6.9, rounded up to 7 lb billable.
  • 10 × 8 × 6 in box, if the product compresses to fit: 480 cubic inches ÷ 139 = 3.45, rounded up to 4 lb billable.

Same product, same 2 lb on the scale, and the billable weight falls from 14 lb to 4 lb just by choosing a better-fitting carton. Dense items behave the opposite way: a 40 lb box of canned goods is billed on its scale weight, because that is the larger number, and the box size barely matters.

The practical rule is that DIM weight punishes light and bulky products (pillows, bedding, apparel, plastic housewares, anything with a lot of air) and mostly ignores small and dense ones.

What shipping zones are

A zone is a band of distance between the origin ZIP code, where the parcel ships from, and the destination ZIP code. For ground service, zones generally run from 2 for nearby destinations up to 8 for the farthest ones in the lower 48. The higher the zone, the more it costs per pound, and the longer the parcel is in transit.

The zone depends on where the parcel starts, not where your company is registered. That makes warehouse location a pricing decision: the same customer in Denver can be in a different zone depending on which of your warehouses their order leaves from.

How the two combine

A carrier rate card is a grid. One axis is billable weight in pounds and the other is the zone. Your postage for any parcel is the cell where the two meet. That means DIM weight moves you down the grid and distance moves you across it, and a bulky parcel going to a distant zone is in the most expensive corner of the table.

It also means the two problems compound. Shipping a 14 lb billable parcel to zone 8 costs far more than a 4 lb billable parcel to zone 4, even though the product inside is identical. We are deliberately not quoting rate-card prices here, because they differ by carrier and contract and are adjusted every year. What does not change is the shape of the grid: heavier and farther is always more.

Why a Houston origin helps with zones

Houston sits near the middle of the country along I-10, I-45 and 610. Shipping from here, ground parcels reach roughly 60% of the US population in two days. Customers in the central and eastern US tend to land in lower zones than they would from a warehouse on either coast, and lower zones mean a lower cell in that grid for every parcel.

We do not claim any particular zone number for any particular city, because that depends on the exact ZIP codes and the carrier. If you want to know where your own customers fall, the right test is to take a sample of last month's orders and look at their destination ZIPs against a Houston origin. A good 3PL will run that comparison for you before you sign anything.

How to lower the DIM part of your bill

  1. Right-size the carton. Measure your best-selling SKUs packed, not the product alone. Cutting even an inch from one dimension on a bulky item can drop a whole pound or more.
  2. Use poly mailers where the product allows. Soft goods in a mailer take far less volume than the same goods in a box.
  3. Cut the void fill. If a box is half air plus crinkle paper, you are paying to ship the air.
  4. Keep a small set of box sizes. Two or three sizes that fit most orders beat one oversized default. Fewer sizes also keep packing quick and consistent.
  5. Check multi-item orders. Two items shipped in one right-sized carton are often billed less than two separate parcels.
  6. Ask your 3PL for billable weight reporting. If your invoice only shows postage, you cannot tell whether the cost came from weight, size or distance.

How to lower the zone part

You have fewer levers here, and most are about where inventory sits. Shipping from a central warehouse reduces the average zone. Splitting inventory across more than one location can cut it further at higher volumes, though it adds inventory complexity and storage cost that only pay off once order volume justifies it. Faster, more expensive services are not the lever: a higher service level raises the price in every zone.
