What Is Devanning? Container Unloading Explained

Devanning is the step between a container arriving and your inventory being sellable. Here is what it involves and what to ask before your first import.

Published 2026-11-16 · 4 min read

Devanning is unloading a shipping container: opening the doors, taking out every carton or pallet, and counting it into a warehouse. For an importer it is the step between "the container arrived" and "the inventory is sellable", and how fast it happens decides whether you pay extra for it.

The word comes from "van", the older term for a container. Loading one is called stuffing; unloading it is devanning (some people say stripping or unstuffing, and it all means the same thing). Here is what it involves and what to ask before your first import lands at the Port of Houston.

What happens during a devan

A container is only useful to you once its contents are counted and on a shelf. A proper devan covers more than unloading:

  • Opening and checking the seal. The seal number should match your paperwork. A mismatch is something you want recorded before anyone touches the cargo.
  • Unloading. Cartons come out by hand or by forklift, depending on how the container was packed.
  • Counting every piece. The count is checked against your packing list, not against the number someone assumes is right.
  • Inspecting condition. Crushed cartons, water damage and shortages are documented, with photos, while the container is still open.
  • Putaway. Stock goes to storage, or straight to Amazon FBA prep or order fulfillment if it is already needed.

The documentation matters because a damage or shortage claim is much harder to win once the container has been closed up and returned. The record made at the dock is your evidence.

Floor-loaded versus palletized

Containers reach a warehouse packed in one of two ways, and it changes the work involved.

Palletized freight is already on pallets, so a forklift can pull it out quickly. Floor-loaded freight, also called loose-loaded, is stacked carton by carton from floor to ceiling. Suppliers often pack this way because it fits more units in the box, but every carton has to be unloaded by hand, and that takes noticeably longer and costs more labor. We devan both. If you have a choice, ask your supplier how the container will be packed before it sails, so that you and your 3PL both know what to expect.

Live unload versus drop

The other split is how the truck behaves at the dock. In a live unload the driver waits while the container is emptied. In a drop, the driver leaves the container on its chassis in the yard and returns later to pick up the empty. Drops free the driver to keep working, but the container sits on a chassis in the meantime, which brings us to the part that costs money.

Why the clock matters: per-diem and detention

Every day a container and its chassis are out of circulation, someone is charging for it. After a free period set by the steamship line and terminal, you owe per-diem on the container, and detention or chassis fees can follow. Delay the devan and those charges accrue every day the box is sitting full and waiting for a slot at a warehouse.

Our guide to importing through the Port of Houston walks through the full path from vessel to shelf and the demurrage and per-diem charges to plan for. The short version is that the fastest way to cut those charges is to shorten the gap between the container clearing and the container being empty.

Who actually devans your container

There are usually three ways it gets done:

  • Your own warehouse or a 3PL that does it in-house. One party moves the container, unloads it and receives the inventory.
  • A transload or cross-dock facility. The container is unloaded at a separate site and the freight is then re-trucked to wherever you actually store it.
  • A trucker who hands off to a warehouse. Often the most fragmented route, with separate invoices and separate handoffs.

Each additional handoff adds a day of delay, another invoice and another party who can lose or damage a carton. A transload also means paying to handle the same freight twice: once to unload it and once to receive it at the destination.

Why Houston importers can skip a step

Most 3PLs sit well inland, which means a container leaving the port has a long truck ride before anyone opens the doors. Our warehouse is close to the Port of Houston, so we can arrange drayage and devanning together: the container comes from the terminal straight to our dock, and we aim to unload, count and shelve it the same day. There is no inland freight leg and no transload in the middle.

Because storage and fulfillment are in the same building, goods can also go from the container to wherever they are needed next. A portion can be prepped to spec for Amazon FBA, with the rest shelved for direct-to-consumer orders, all from the same container.

What to ask before your first container

If you are about to bring in your first import, put these questions to whoever will devan it:

  1. Do you arrange the drayage and chassis, or do I book those separately?
  2. Do you handle floor-loaded containers, and is the labor priced differently from palletized?
  3. How soon after the container reaches your dock is the inventory counted and available?
  4. How are damage and shortages documented, and how quickly will I hear about them?
  5. Can part of the container go straight to FBA prep or fulfillment?

A good answer to the last four is specific. A vague one usually means there is a handoff the provider has not mentioned. If you are still choosing a partner more generally, how to choose a 3PL covers the wider checklist.
