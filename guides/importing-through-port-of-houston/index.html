Importing Through the Port of Houston: A Guide for E-commerce Brands

How your goods move from a vessel at the Port of Houston to your customers — customs, drayage, devanning, and the demurrage fees to avoid — plus why importing here ships faster.

Published 2026-08-107 min read

Your first overseas container is a rite of passage for a growing e-commerce brand — and often the first time logistics stops being simple. Between the vessel and your customer sits a chain of customs filings, terminal schedules, trucking, and unloading, each with its own clock and its own fees. Importing through the Port of Houston is where a lot of Texas and national brands start, and for good reason: it is the leading U.S. port for foreign waterborne tonnage, and 2025 was its best year on record, with container volume (TEUs) up about 4% over the prior year.

This guide walks the real path a container takes from the water to your shelf, the paperwork and players you will deal with along the way, and the charges that quietly eat margin if you are not ready for them. It also explains why finishing that journey in Houston — rather than trucking your freight to a distant coast — tends to be both faster and cheaper.

Why import through the Port of Houston

The Port of Houston isn't just big; it's positioned. It's the number-one U.S. port for foreign waterborne tonnage and the busiest container port on the Gulf Coast, moving cargo through two dedicated container terminals, Barbours Cut and Bayport. That scale means deep, reliable sailing schedules and ample chassis and drayage capacity — the practical things that decide whether your container moves on day one or sits for a week.

It's also getting bigger. Project 11, the roughly $1 billion widening and deepening of the Houston Ship Channel, lets larger vessels pass more safely and in both directions; Port Houston completed its own dredging portion of the project in 2025. For importers, more capacity upstream means fewer bottlenecks downstream.

Then there's geography. Houston sits in the center of the country, so goods that land here reach about 60% of the U.S. population in one to two days by ground — and Texas levies no state income tax, which trims both your landed cost and your logistics partner's operating cost versus a coastal hub. Pair the port with port-direct drayage and devanning and an entire inland freight leg disappears.

The import journey, step by step

Every import follows roughly the same arc. Knowing the sequence tells you where the delays — and the fees — tend to hide:

  • Booking and departure: your supplier or freight forwarder books space on a vessel and you receive a bill of lading. An Importer Security Filing (ISF, or "10+2") must be filed at least 24 hours before the container is loaded overseas.
  • Sailing and arrival: the vessel crosses and berths at a Houston container terminal, where your container is discharged from the ship.
  • Customs clearance: U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) reviews your entry — commercial invoice, packing list, tariff classification and duties. Cleared cargo is released; flagged cargo can be held for exam.
  • Availability and drayage: once released and unloaded to the yard, the container becomes available for pickup, and a drayage carrier trucks it from the terminal to your warehouse.
  • Devanning and receiving: the container is unloaded ("devanned"), every carton is counted and inspected, and your inventory is logged and shelved — or sent straight into prep and fulfillment.
  • Fulfillment: from there it's picking, packing and shipping to your customers, or forwarding a portion to a marketplace like Amazon.

The paperwork and players you'll meet

You don't have to master customs law to import, but you should know who does what. A freight forwarder arranges the ocean move and documentation. A licensed customs broker files your entry with CBP, classifies your goods under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS), and calculates duties — most importers use one. You'll also need a customs bond, a guarantee that duties and fees will be paid, which can be single-entry or continuous; brands importing regularly usually take a continuous bond.

The documents that keep it all moving are your commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading and ISF. Accurate HTS codes matter more than they look: they set your duty rate, and a wrong classification can mean overpaying for years or facing penalties down the line. When in doubt, this is where a good broker earns the fee.

Demurrage and detention: the fees that punish delay

The costs that surprise first-time importers usually aren't the duties — they're the storage penalties. Demurrage is charged when your container sits at the terminal past its allotted free time, waiting to be picked up. Detention (also called per-diem) is charged when you've pulled the container out but haven't returned the empty box to the port in time. Both accrue daily, and during congested stretches they add up fast.

The way to beat them is speed at the dock: have drayage arranged before the container is even available, and devan it the day it arrives so the empty goes back quickly. That's the whole logic of same-day devanning, and why receiving your container close to the port instead of trucking it inland first protects your margin. Flexible warehousing and storage on the other side means the goods have somewhere to land the moment they're counted.

From the terminal to your customer's doorstep

Clearing the port is only half the job; the point is getting products to buyers. Because devanning, storage and fulfillment can all happen in one building minutes from the terminal, a container that clears in the morning can be counted, shelved, and shipping direct-to-consumer orders the same day — often within 48 hours of the vessel berthing. Sellers who route to marketplaces can have a portion prepped to Amazon FBA spec and forwarded from the same stock.

That's the quiet advantage of importing through Houston: the freight is already where it needs to be to reach the country quickly.
